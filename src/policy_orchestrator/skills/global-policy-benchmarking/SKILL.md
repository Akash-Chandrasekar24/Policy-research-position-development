# Global Policy Benchmarking

Naming a foreign law is not benchmarking. This skill requires an honest
comparison of institutional context, not just outcomes.

## Method

1. **Select jurisdictions deliberately.** Choose 2-4 that are genuinely
   comparable on at least one relevant axis: market size, legal tradition
   (common law vs. civil law), regulatory maturity, or economic
   development stage. State why each one was chosen.
2. **Describe the actual approach**, not just its label — e.g. not "the EU
   has an AI Act" but what the Act actually requires (risk tiers,
   obligations, enforcement mechanism).
3. **Identify measurable outcomes** where available — compliance costs,
   enforcement actions, market effects, litigation — not just stated
   intentions.
4. **State the honest strength and limitation of each model** — every
   approach has a real trade-off; naming only strengths (or only
   criticisms) is a sign of cherry-picking.
5. **Assess India-relevance explicitly**: does India have the institutional
   capacity, market structure, or legal tradition for this model to work
   the same way? A model requiring strong independent regulators may not
   transfer to a context where those regulators don't yet exist with the
   same independence.
6. **Cite the literal source URL** for each jurisdiction's approach (the
   statute, regulator's page, or official summary) — a bare "the EU AI Act"
   is not a citation.

## Output format

Table: `Jurisdiction | Approach (source URL) | Strength | Limitation | India relevance (High/Partial/Low + why)`

## Common failure modes to avoid

- Picking jurisdictions because they're famous (EU, US) rather than because
  they're comparable.
- Reporting a foreign law's stated goals as if they were its proven
  outcomes.
- Concluding "India should adopt the EU model" without addressing why the
  EU's institutional starting point differs from India's.
- Naming a law without linking to it — the office needs to verify the
  actual text, not just the label.
